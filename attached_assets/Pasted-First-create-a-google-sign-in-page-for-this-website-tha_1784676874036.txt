This approach works for any two points in space.




Circle formula:
The Pythagorean theorem is also closely related to circles. Mathematically, a circle is just every point that is a set distance away from a center point. How would we write an equation describing all points that have a distance r, from a certain point?

The screen transitions to a fresh coordinate grid. A movable center point appears on the grid labeled (x1, y1). A perfect circle outlines itself around this point. A solid radius line segment connects the center to a moveable point on the perimeter labeled (x2, y2). When the user drags the center point, the entire circle shifts. When the user drags the perimeter point, the radius line rotates smoothly around the center.
The text updates: Let's use the distance formula we derived earlier: d = square root of ((x2 - x1)^2 + (y2 - y1)^2). Since we want the distance from one single point to be constant, let's set d to be any arbitrary constant value, let's say 5.
The general distance formula fades onto the screen directly above the graph. The character d changes into the number 5, while the label on the physical radius line on the graph also switches from r to 5. The equation now reads: 5 = square root of ((x2 - x1)^2 + (y2 - y1)^2).
The text updates: Now, if we fix our “center point” (x1,y1) to be constants, lets say (2,3), every point (x2,y2) that satisfies this equation must be on the circle of radius 5 centered at 2,3.
On the graph, the center point slides cleanly to the position (2, 3). In the equation, the variables x1 and y1 fade out, and the numbers 2 and 3 slide into their empty slots inside the brackets. The equation updates to: 5 = square root of ((x2 - 2)^2 + (y2 - 3)^2).
The text updates: To simplify this and remove the radical, we square both sides. We can also change x2 and y2 to standard x and y, since this applies to any general point on the boundary.
In the animation, the large square root symbol vanishes. Also, a superscript 2 slides onto the left side to turn the 5 into 5^2. The small 2 subscripts on the x and y variables shrink and disappear. The line reorganizes into: 5^2 = (x - 2)^2 + (y - 3)^2.
A dashed right triangle projects itself inside the circle, spanning between the center (2, 3) and the perimeter point (x, y). The expression (x - 2) slides from the equation and labels the horizontal leg, the expression (y - 3) slides down to label the vertical leg, and the number 5 labels the hypotenuse.
The final text concludes: We have just derived the equation for a circle centered at (2, 3) with a radius of 5. To generalize this to any center coordinate (h, k) and any radius r, the equation takes the standard form: (x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2. The equation of a circle is simply the distance formula with a fixed radius, and the distance formula is simply the Pythagorean theorem in disguise.
At the very bottom of the screen, the classic equation a^2 + b^2 = c^2 fades in directly beneath the general circle formula. Brackets smoothly draw themselves around the terms to align them vertically, showing that (x - h)^2 maps to a^2, (y - k)^2 maps to b^2, and r^2 maps to c^2.
